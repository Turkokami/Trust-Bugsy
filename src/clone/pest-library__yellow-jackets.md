# Yellow Jackets

Pest Library

## Yellow Jackets

Vespula species

Fast, bold, black-and-yellow wasps that nest by the thousands in the ground or wall voids and turn aggressive scavengers by late summer — the stinging insect most likely to send someone to urgent care.

## How to identify them

Yellow jackets are compact, roughly half-inch wasps with crisp black-and-yellow banding and a fast, darting flight. Unlike the hairy, lumbering honey bee, they are smooth and quick. Their nests are enclosed in a papery shell but usually hidden — in ground cavities, wall voids, under structures, or in dense shrubs — so the nest itself is often not seen until it is disturbed.

## Behavior and biology in South Florida

A queen starts the colony in spring, and by late summer a single nest can hold hundreds to thousands of workers. Early in the year they hunt insects; as the colony peaks and natural food dwindles, they shift to scavenging sugars and proteins — which is why they swarm trash cans, sodas, and picnics in the fall.

Yellow jackets are the most defensive of the common stinging insects. A vibrating lawnmower, foot traffic over a ground nest, or a hand near a void entrance can trigger a mass defensive response, and each wasp can sting many times.

## Signs of an infestation

- Steady traffic of wasps in and out of a hole in the ground, wall, or slab
- Wasps swarming trash, food, and drinks in late summer
- A hidden nest revealed by mowing, gardening, or renovation
- Rapid, aggressive response when the nest area is disturbed

## Why they are a problem

Yellow jackets account for a large share of serious stinging incidents. Because their nests are hidden and their colonies huge, people often stumble onto them, provoking dozens of stings — a medical emergency for anyone allergic and painful for everyone else. Nests in wall voids can also push wasps into living space.

## Preventing yellow jackets

- Keep trash cans sealed and clean, especially in late summer
- Cover food and drinks at outdoor gatherings
- Seal gaps in siding, soffits, and around utility penetrations that lead to voids
- Watch for ground-nest traffic before mowing or gardening
- Never seal or plug an active nest yourself — call a professional
