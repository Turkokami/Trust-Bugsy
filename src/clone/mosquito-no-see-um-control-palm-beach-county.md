## Why mosquito and no-see-um pressure peaks here in summer

Palm Beach County’s rainy season runs roughly May through October, and that’s exactly when biting insects explode. Daily afternoon downpours leave standing water in gutters, bromeliads, plant saucers, tarps, boat covers, bird baths, and clogged drains — and a female mosquito needs only a bottle-cap of water to lay a raft of eggs. Within a week that water becomes a new generation of biters.

Our position between the Atlantic and the Everglades makes it worse. Salt-marsh mosquitoes breed in the mangroves and tidal flats along the Intracoastal and hatch by the thousands after king tides, then fly inland for miles. No-see-ums — biting midges so small they slip through standard window and pool-cage screens — swarm at dawn and dusk near the water, canals, and mangroves. A single untreated property can stay miserable even when the neighborhood is otherwise calm.

- Standing water after storms is the number-one driver — source reduction matters as much as spraying.
- Salt-marsh mosquitoes are strong fliers; waterfront and Intracoastal homes feel the brunt after high tides.
- No-see-ums are most active at dawn and dusk and pass through ordinary screens.
- Warm nights let mosquitoes stay active nearly year-round in South Florida — pressure eases in winter but rarely stops.

## The biters we target across the county

Different species bite at different times and breed in different places, so effective control means treating for all of them — not just fogging and hoping.

### Aedes aegypti (container mosquitoes)

Aggressive daytime biters that breed in tiny containers right around the home. The primary local concern for dengue and Zika, so we focus hard on source reduction.

### Culex (house mosquitoes)

Dusk-and-dawn biters that breed in stagnant, organic-rich water like clogged drains and neglected pools. The main West Nile virus vector in Florida.

### Salt-marsh mosquitoes

Powerful fliers that hatch from mangroves and tidal flats after king tides and travel inland. Why waterfront yards flare up seemingly overnight.

### No-see-ums (biting midges)

Nearly invisible, intensely itchy, and small enough to pass through standard screens. Worst near the water at dawn and dusk — they need a targeted approach, not just mosquito fogging.

## Frequently asked questions

### When is mosquito season in Palm Beach County?
Practically speaking, it never fully stops here — but pressure ramps up sharply with the rainy season from about May through October and peaks in the humid, storm-heavy months of summer. Warm winters keep some activity going year-round, which is why many waterfront and wetland-adjacent homes stay on a program even in the cooler months.

### What’s the difference between mosquitoes and no-see-ums — and why do no-see-ums get through my screens?
No-see-ums are biting midges, a different (and much smaller) insect than mosquitoes. They’re tiny enough to pass through standard window and pool-cage screening, which is why you can be bitten on a “screened” porch. They’re worst at dawn and dusk near water and mangroves. Because they breed and rest differently than mosquitoes, we treat for them specifically rather than relying on mosquito fogging alone.

### How soon will I notice fewer bites after a treatment?
Most homeowners notice a clear drop within a day or two as the adult population is knocked down. The lasting difference comes from the ongoing program — removing breeding sites and maintaining the barrier through the season so new generations don’t simply take over.

### Is mosquito and no-see-um treatment safe for my kids, pets, and pollinators?
Yes, when it’s done responsibly. We use EPA-registered products applied by a Florida-licensed technician, target the shaded resting areas mosquitoes actually use rather than blanketing your whole yard, and avoid treating blooming plants that pollinators visit. We’ll let you know how long to stay off treated areas — typically until dry.

### Do I really need recurring service, or is one treatment enough?
One treatment helps for an event or a bad week, but South Florida’s constant breeding conditions mean new adults keep emerging. A recurring 21–30 day program is what actually keeps a yard usable through the wet season, because it maintains the barrier and stays ahead of each new hatch.

### What can I do between visits to cut down mosquitoes myself?
Dump or refresh standing water at least weekly — saucers, bird baths, buckets, tarps, boat covers, and clogged gutters are the usual culprits. Keep gutters and drains flowing, change pet water and bird baths often, run fountains or aerators so ornamental water stays moving, and trim dense, shady landscaping where adults rest. These simple habits make our treatments last noticeably longer.
